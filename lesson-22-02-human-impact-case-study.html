🔍 Part 1What Is a Case Study?

A case study is a close look at one real situation. Scientists pick one place, one event, or one ecosystem. They study it carefully to understand what changed and why. A case study asks: What happened here? What caused it? What might help?

📁 Our case: The Shrinking Meadow

Before: Tall grass and wildflowers. Bees drank nectar from the flowers. Rabbits hid in the tall grass. Birds nested nearby.

What changed: Half the meadow was paved for a parking lot.

After: Fewer flowers grew near the edge. Bees had less food. Rabbits had fewer places to hide. Some birds stopped nesting there.

Proposed solution: Plant a border of native wildflowers and grasses along the edge of the lot.

This meadow is an example, built from what scientists see in many real places.

🏗️ Part 2When Humans Change a Place

People change ecosystems when they build, farm, travel, or throw things away. When a habitat changes, organisms may lose food, water, shelter, or space.

Then the population in that place can change. Some animals leave, some struggle, and some can't survive there at all.

🎬 Behind the scenes · Who is watching?
This young red-tailed hawk was hunting in a grassland in Wyoming. Hawks have sharp eyes and watch open ground from above. That's why tall grass matters so much to rabbits and mice: it is their shelter.

💡 Part 3Solutions Need Evidence

People can also design solutions that help an ecosystem recover. A good solution is based on evidence, not just guessing. It should reduce harm and help organisms find what they need again.

🎬 Director's cut

Roads that grow wildflowers

Some states use the land along highways as a giant flower border. Texas plants wildflowers beside its roads, and the state says it buys and sows about 30,000 pounds of wildflower seed every year.

Way back in 1934, Texas road crews were told to wait to mow until spring wildflowers were done blooming. Bees and butterflies use those roadside flowers for food.

⭐ Key idea: Human actions can change ecosystems in ways that make survival harder. When a habitat changes, organisms can lose food, water, shelter, or space. Scientists study one case at a time, and use evidence to decide which solutions might help.
