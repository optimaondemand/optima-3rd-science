🌿 Part 1What Is an Ecosystem?

Every national park is an ecosystem. It includes every plant, animal, and other organism that lives there, plus nonliving things like water, soil, temperature, and sunlight. These parts interact all the time. A tree shades the soil. A frog eats insects and gets eaten by a heron. Rain fills a pond. When scientists study a park, they look at the habitat, the organisms, and the environmental conditions together.

🐊 Part 2Who Can Survive Here?

Not every organism can survive in every place. In any habitat, some organisms survive well, some survive less well, and some cannot survive at all. An alligator could not live in a snowy mountain forest, but in the warm, wet Everglades it does just fine. South Florida is the only place on Earth where alligators and crocodiles live side by side.

🎬 Behind the scenes · Sharing data
In Glacier National Park, scientists catch birds in soft nets, record data, and let them go. They share their data with scientists across North America, so everyone can learn how birds are doing.

📣 Part 3Protecting Parks, and Sharing What We Learn

Droughts, floods, wildfires, and hurricanes can change a park quickly. People change parks too, with pollution, building, and invasive species. That's why conservation matters.

Scientists and rangers collect evidence: observations and measurements of the organisms and conditions. Then they communicate. They share their findings in reports, talks, and maps, so other scientists can check the results and park managers can use them to protect the park.

🎬 Director's cut

The Salamander Capital

Great Smoky Mountains National Park is called the Salamander Capital of the World. About 30 kinds live in its cool, wet forests.

Scientists and volunteers count salamanders year after year and share what they find, so everyone can see if the forest is staying healthy.

⭐ Key idea: Every national park is an ecosystem: living organisms and nonliving conditions like water, temperature, and sunlight, all interacting. In any habitat, some organisms survive well, some survive less well, and some cannot survive at all. Scientists collect evidence by observing and measuring, then share their findings so others can check them and use them to protect the park. Protecting a habitat protects all the organisms that depend on it.
