🌲 Part 1Two Very Different Homes

A mountain forest and an open grassland are very different places to live. In a forest, tall trees block much of the sunlight. The soil is rocky and stays damp. It can get very cold.

Out in a grassland, sunlight is everywhere. Wind blows steadily. Rain comes and goes, and the soil can dry out for weeks.

🍃 Part 2Traits Are Solutions

A fern on the shady forest floor has wide, flat fronds. Those broad leaves catch every bit of light that filters through the trees. That trait is a solution to the problem of shade.

A prairie grass has thin, tough leaves and roots that go deep, sometimes deeper than you are tall. That root system is a solution to dry seasons. It can reach water far underground when the surface dries out.

🎬 Behind the scenes · Built for wind
Big bluestem, a prairie grass, can grow taller than a grown-up. Scientists say its long, thin leaves and stems bend in the prairie wind instead of breaking. Its seed head splits into three parts, so people nicknamed it "turkey foot."

⭐ Part 3The Right Trait for the Right Place

A trait gives an advantage when it solves a problem in that specific place. Deep roots are an advantage in a dry grassland. They are far less useful in a wet forest, where water is already near the surface.

Plants with traits that fit their place survive and make seeds or spores. Plants without them often don't.

🎬 Director's cut

A prairie's hidden half

Scientist John Weaver spent years digging up prairie plants, root and all. He found that most of a prairie is hidden underground.

Scientists have measured switchgrass roots, a prairie grass, 8 to 11 feet deep. That's about as deep as a basketball hoop is high!

⭐ Key idea: A trait is an adaptation when it gives a plant a survival advantage in its environment. The same trait that helps a plant in one place may be useless, or even harmful, somewhere else.
