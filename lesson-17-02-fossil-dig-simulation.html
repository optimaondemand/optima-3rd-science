👀 Part 1Observation Comes First

Before a paleontologist explains anything, she looks carefully and writes down exactly what she sees. That is called recording data.

An observation is only what your senses tell you. "The fossil has ridges" is an observation. "It came from a shellfish" is already an inference.

🐾 Part 2From Data to Inference

Next, scientists analyze the data. They ask: What patterns do I see? What does this remind me of in living things today?

A strong inference names the clue that supports it. "This animal may have eaten plants because its teeth are flat and wide" is strong. "This animal looked weird" is not.

🎬 Behind the scenes · Even cracks turn to stone
These cracks formed when wet mud dried out in the sun, more than a billion years ago, in what is now Glacier National Park, Montana. New mud covered them, and they turned to stone. The rock itself is evidence: this place was wet, and then it dried out.

🪨 Part 3The Rock Is Data, Too

For a fossil to form, a living thing must be buried quickly in sediment: sand, mud, or volcanic ash. Over millions of years, the sediment hardens into rock.

The kind of rock is a clue. Dark mudstone often forms in wet, swampy places. Pale limestone can form in calm, shallow water. Gray-green shale forms in quiet water where fine mud settles.

🎬 Director's cut

The oldest fossils on Earth

These lumpy mounds are called stromatolites. Tiny living things, too small to see, build them layer by layer in shallow sea water.

Scientists think fossils of mounds like these are among the oldest fossils ever found: about 3.5 billion years old, in rock in Australia. That's long before plants, fish, or dinosaurs.

⭐ Key idea: Scientists observe first and record exact data. Then they make an inference that points back to that data. The rock around a fossil is data too, and more clues that agree make a stronger inference.
