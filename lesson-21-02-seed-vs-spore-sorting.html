🍃 Part 1Some Plants Use Spores

Ferns, mosses, and some other plants never make seeds. They reproduce with spores. A spore is just one tiny cell with a thin, tough wall. You often can't see spores without a magnifying glass.

Spores form in small clusters on the underside of fern leaves. The fern releases them, and they drift away on air or water. Most land in the wrong place, so scientists estimate one fern can release millions of spores. A spore that lands somewhere damp first grows into a tiny green heart-shaped plant. A new fern grows from that heart.

🌰 Part 2Some Plants Use Seeds

Many plants reproduce using seeds. A seed is a package. Inside its tough seed coat is a tiny baby plant, called an embryo, and stored food to help it start growing. A small scar, the hilum, shows where the seed was attached to its parent.

Seeds come from flowers, fruits, and cones. A seed that lands in a good spot, with water and warmth, can start to grow.

🔬 Part 3Scientists Classify with Evidence

How do scientists tell seeds and spores apart? They look at the evidence: size, where it came from, and what is inside. Botanists, scientists who study plants, sort plant parts this way.

Sometimes there isn't enough evidence to be sure. Then scientists write "not enough evidence" instead of guessing. That's more honest, and more useful.

🎬 Director's cut

A 2,000-year-old seed that grew

Long ago, people stored dates in a desert fortress in Israel. Scientists found some of the seeds. Tests showed they were about 2,000 years old. In 2005, scientists planted one, and it sprouted!

How? A seed is a package. Its tough coat kept the baby plant safe, and its stored food was still there when the seed finally got water.

⭐ Key idea: Seeds and spores both help plants reproduce. A seed carries a baby plant and stored food inside a coat. A spore is just one tiny cell, with no baby plant and no stored food. Scientists use size, where it came from, and what's inside as evidence to tell them apart.
