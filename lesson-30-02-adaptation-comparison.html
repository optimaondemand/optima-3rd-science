🏆 Part 2When a Small Difference Matters

Most variations don't change much. But some do. In Florida scrub, fires sweep through every few years. A tortoise that digs a deeper burrow stays cooler underground, so its variation is a survival advantage.

Survivors have young, and the young can inherit the helpful characteristic. Scientists think this is how, over many generations, a whole population changes.

🔬 Part 3Evidence and Animal Groups

Scientists use evidence: observations and measurements, like a mouse's fur color or how deep a burrow goes. They also sort animals into groups. Mammals have fur, and most give birth to live young. Birds have feathers and lay eggs. Reptiles have dry scales. Amphibians have smooth, wet skin. Fish have gills. All of these are vertebrates, animals with a backbone. Insects have no backbone.

🎬 Director's cut

Burrow City

A gopher tortoise burrow can be as long as a school bus. Deep inside, it stays cool in summer and safe during fires.

Scientists have found more than 350 kinds of animals using these burrows: snakes, frogs, mice, insects, and more.

⭐ Key idea: Individuals in a population are not all the same. These small differences are called variations. Most variations don't matter much, but some give an individual a survival advantage, like a deeper burrow during a fire or pale fur on white sand. Survivors have young, and the young can inherit the helpful characteristic. Scientists use evidence, like observations and measurements, to find out which variations matter.
